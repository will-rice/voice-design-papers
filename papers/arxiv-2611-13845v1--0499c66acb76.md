Alania-2

Turkish text-to-speech  
with instant and fine-tuned voice cloning

Sezgin Saygili  Emre Kaplaner  Oncel Ozgul  Fikri San Koktas

PatientDesk
AI [sezgin@patientdesk.ai](mailto:sezgin@patientdesk.ai) [speech.patientdesk.ai](https://speech.patientdesk.ai)

 

Abstract

We present Alania-2, a Turkish text-to-speech (TTS) system with two
built-in voices, voice design from text descriptions, and two kinds of
voice cloning. Alania-2 is a 488M-parameter model with the VoxCPM
architecture, which generates continuous audio latents with a diffusion
head on top of a language-model backbone. We train it from scratch on
18,195 hours of Turkish speech, 90% of which is synthetic speech
produced by a larger teacher model and filtered automatically. A text
normalizer rewrites numbers, abbreviations and foreign names into the
words a Turkish speaker would say; without it, none of 96 test sentences
with digits kept all its critical parts, such as an amount or a date,
through synthesis and speech recognition, against 86 with it. On
Freya-TR-Eval, a public benchmark of 495 Turkish sentences scored with
automatic speech recognition, Alania-2 reaches a word error rate of
1.44%, compared with 5.99% for our previous system. On its built-in
voices, it comes within half a point of character error rate of a
2.3B-parameter model at under a quarter of the size, and a
7.6M-parameter single-voice model trained with the same recipe matches
the most intelligible systems we measured, which suggests that training
data matters more for intelligibility than model size. We also report
practical findings on voice cloning: conditioning on the reference audio
alone, rather than continuing it, cuts skipped words from 21.9% to 1.7%;
20–30 seconds of reference audio works best; and a low-rank adapter
trained in about two minutes raises speaker similarity well above
instant cloning. Finally, we describe inference-engine changes that
bring first audio down to 32 ms for a single stream. We release our
evaluation harness and three Turkish speech and text datasets.

Keywords: text-to-speech; Turkish; voice cloning; synthetic training
data; text normalization; low-latency inference

 

## 1  Introduction

Turkish is spoken by more than eighty million people, but it remains a
mid-resource language for speech synthesis. Recent zero-shot TTS models
(Chen et al., 2025a; Du et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025b; Zhou et al., 2025) were trained mainly on English and Chinese speech. Multilingual
successors such as VoxCPM2 (Zhou et al., 2026) support Turkish as one of
30 languages, and open Turkish systems differ by an order of magnitude
in intelligibility (Section 6).

Turkish text is also hard to read aloud correctly. Numbers, dates and
foreign names take case suffixes that must agree with how they are
pronounced, and the dotted and dotless _i_ distinguish words but are
easily broken by case conversion designed for English.

We built Alania-2 for telephone voice assistants and long-form
narration. These uses need reliable reading of numbers, doses, dates and
names; low latency when streaming; consistent built-in voices; and voice
cloning that customers can use on their own. Our previous system,
Alania-1, is a 3.3B-parameter language model over 24 kHz SNAC codec
tokens (Siuzdak et al., 2024). It misreads about one word in seventeen
on the Freya-TR-Eval benchmark (Pamuk et al., 2026).

Figure 1 gives an overview of Alania-2. Our contributions are:

1.  1
    A data recipe for a mid-resource language. We train a 488M-parameter
    model from scratch on 90% synthetic speech and show that, on its
    built-in voices, it comes within half a point of character error
    rate of a fine-tuned model more than four times its size (Sections 3
    and 5).
2.  2
    A text normalizer with 134 readings and a lexicon reviewed by a
    native-speaker author, and respelling of foreign brand names
    (Section 4).
3.  3
    A comparison with 14 other systems under one protocol (Section 6).
4.  4
    Evidence that model size matters little for intelligibility. A
    2.3B-parameter model trained on our data is barely more intelligible
    than Alania-2, and a 7.6M-parameter single-voice model trained with
    the same recipe matches the best systems we measured (Section 7).
5.  5
    Practical findings on voice cloning: why continuing a reference
    skips words, how much reference audio helps, and fast fine-tuning
    with low-rank adapters (Section 8).
6.  6
    Inference-engine changes for low latency and for serving many
    fine-tuned voices from one model (Section 10).

We release the Freya-TR-Eval harness and results, a 2,398-hour Turkish
caption set, a 3,411-hour synthetic speech set and a 1.44M-sentence
domain text corpus. We do not release the model weights.

## 2  Related Work

#### Zero-shot TTS and voice cloning.

Codec language models clone a voice by continuing a short audio prompt
(Chen et al., 2025a). CosyVoice (Du et al., 2024) combines supervised
semantic tokens with flow matching, and F5-TTS (Chen et al., 2025b)
removes the need for explicit text–speech alignment. VoxCPM (Zhou
et al., 2025) predicts continuous latents of an audio variational
autoencoder (VAE) with a diffusion decoder on a language-model backbone,
without a discrete speech tokenizer. VoxCPM2 (Zhou et al., 2026) extends
it to 30 languages, voice design from text and a VAE that encodes at
16 kHz and decodes at 48 kHz. We use the VoxCPM2 architecture, tokenizer
and VAE but train our own weights. Our previous system, Alania-1,
followed the codec route with SNAC tokens (Siuzdak et al., 2024).

#### Turkish TTS.

Yeshpanov et al. (2023) train a Tacotron 2 model on Kazakh alone and
synthesize nine other Turkic languages, including Turkish, by mapping
their letters through the International Phonetic Alphabet to Kazakh
letters. FreyaTTS (Pamuk et al., 2026) is a 183M-parameter Turkish-first
flow-matching model released together with the Freya-TR-Eval benchmark,
and Ozkose et al. (2026) compare four recent systems on Turkish,
fine-tuned and zero-shot, with eighteen objective metrics.

#### Data and evaluation tools.

We build our real-speech set from YODAS (Li et al., 2023), a
multilingual corpus of YouTube audio, much of it with manual or
automatic subtitles. We filter it with a language classifier trained on
VoxLingua107 (Valk and Alumäe, 2021), the AST audio tagger (Gong et al.,
2021), DNSMOS quality prediction (Reddy et al., 2021) and Whisper speech
recognition (Radford et al., 2023). Our synthetic text comes from
FineWeb2 (Penedo et al., 2025), Wikipedia and sentences written by Qwen3
(Yang et al., 2025). For evaluation, we measure intelligibility with
Whisper, naturalness with UTMOS (Saeki et al., 2022), and speaker
similarity with ECAPA-TDNN embeddings (Desplanques et al., 2020;
Ravanelli et al., 2021); FLEURS (Conneau et al., 2023) supplies some of
the cloning references.

#### Efficient serving.

PagedAttention (Kwon et al., 2023) manages the key/value cache of
language models in fixed-size blocks, and S-LoRA (Sheng et al., 2024)
and Punica (Chen et al., 2024) serve many low-rank adapters (LoRA; Hu
et al., 2022) on one base model. Our engine builds on Nano-vLLM
(GeeeekExplorer, 2025; a710128, 2025), which uses the same block-based
cache.

## 3  Data

Figure 2 summarizes the training data. The base mix combines 1,772 hours
of filtered real speech with 16,423 hours of synthetic speech; a second,
shorter stage adds voice design and style instructions.

### 3.1  Real speech

We start from the Turkish part of YODAS (Li et al., 2023): 38,494
recordings totalling 9,216 hours. Most of this audio is weakly labelled.
Of the recordings, 97% carry automatic YouTube captions, and only 2,448
hours are labelled as Turkish at all. We therefore treat every file as
untrusted. We split it into segments, check the language with a
classifier trained on VoxLingua107 (Valk and Alumäe, 2021) and with
Whisper (Radford et al., 2023), remove music and singing with the AST
audio tagger (Gong et al., 2021), reject clipped or band-limited audio,
and keep segments with a DNSMOS score of at least 2.4 (Reddy et al.,
2021). Finally, we transcribe every segment again with Duyu, our Turkish
speech recognizer, a fine-tune of Whisper large-v3 turbo.

This leaves 2,398 hours (835,267 segments, median length 9.9 s).
Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025) writes a short style caption for each
segment, describing the speaker and the delivery. For training, we apply
stricter filters and remove segments with a low DNSMOS score (189,631),
a Duyu transcript that disagrees with the YouTube caption (42,186), or
an implausible speaking rate (152). The training set holds 598,213
segments (1,772 hours) from 19,969 speaker clusters; we hold out about
1% of the clusters for validation.

### 3.2  Synthetic speech

Turkish speech with the quality, speaker balance and text coverage we
needed is not available at this scale, so we generate it with VoxCPM2
(Zhou et al., 2026), using 10 diffusion steps and a guidance scale of
2.0. One H100 GPU produces 83 seconds of audio per second.

#### Text.

The text comes from FineWeb2 (Penedo et al., 2025) (40%), domain
sentences written by Qwen3-30B-A3B (Yang et al., 2025) (25%), Wikipedia
(22.5%) and YODAS transcripts (12.5%). After removing duplicates, 5.65M
of 6.18M text units remain. Each unit is normalized (Section 4) with the
normalizer version current at the time.

#### Voices.

The speakers are our two built-in voices and a bank of 2,752 designed
voices (1,283 female, 1,469 male). We create the bank from 5,000 text
descriptions and drop near-duplicates whose speaker embeddings have a
cosine similarity of 0.85 or more. Each clip is generated in one of
three modes: continuing a reference recording (77%), following a
reference together with an English style instruction (20%), or designing
a voice from its description alone (3%). About 22.5% of the training
clips are also passed through a simulated room, microphone, noise and
codec channel.

#### Quality control.

We keep a clip only if it passes every check in Table 1. In a manual
audit of 681 generated clips, 79.2% passed.

| Check           | Measured with                     | Threshold                                              |
| --------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Intelligibility | CER, faster-whisper large-v3      | $`\leq`$ 0.05                                          |
| Speaking rate   | characters per second             | 7–25                                                   |
| Voice match     | ECAPA similarity to the reference | $`\geq`$ 0.45 ($`\geq`$ 0.35 with a style instruction) |
| Naturalness     | UTMOS22                           | $`\geq`$ 2.5                                           |
| Pitch           | median F0                         | consistent with the voice’s gender                     |

Table 1: Checks applied to every synthetic clip. A clip is kept only if
it passes all of them.

## 4  Text Normalization

Alania-2 reads Turkish spelling and has no phoneme input, so numbers,
symbols, abbreviations and foreign words must first be rewritten into
the words a speaker would say. Table 2 shows typical cases. Our
normalizer is a sequence of rules for numbers, money, dates, times,
ranges, phone numbers, doses, units, titles, addresses and symbols.
After expanding a number, an amount or a unit, it re-attaches the
Turkish suffix that followed it and adjusts the suffix to the new word,
mainly its vowels (vowel harmony). A companion paper describes the
normalizer in detail and evaluates its version of 7 October 2026, which
has 131 test cases; here we summarize the parts that matter most for the
model.

| Case                     | Written     | Spoken form given to the model |
| ------------------------ | ----------- | ------------------------------ |
| Money with a suffix      | 750 TL'dir  | yedi yüz elli liradır          |
| Decimal amount           | 12,50 TL    | on iki lira elli kuruş         |
| Percentage with a suffix | %2,5'lik    | yüzde iki buçukluk             |
| Turkish initialism       | SGK         | segeka                         |
| Borrowed initialism      | CEO         | siio                           |
| Brand name               | PatientDesk | Peyşınt Desk                   |
| Brand name               | ChatGPT     | Çet Ci Pi Ti                   |

Table 2: Examples of written input and the text Alania-2 receives.

#### Initialisms and native review.

Turkish initialisms are usually read as Turkish letter names joined into
one word. Some are read as words, and a few borrowed ones use English
letter names. A native-speaker author reviewed 134 written forms in 13
categories, initialisms among them, on a page that showed each planned
reading as text. The author accepted 118 as shown and changed 16: 10
marked wrong and 6 marked right with a better reading typed in.

#### Upper case.

Words written entirely in capitals are lowercased with Turkish rules,
and every remaining _I_ then becomes _ı_ and every _İ_ becomes _i_.
Other capitals stay. A model that keeps capital letters may otherwise
read a capital _I_ the English way: in an earlier test of ours, VoxCPM2
read the city name _Iğdır_ as _iğdir_.

#### Foreign words and brands.

Read with Turkish spelling rules, some brand names and English words
come out garbled. A word seen at least 1,000 times in the text behind
our 225,764-word Turkish word list stays as written. Otherwise we
respell it in Turkish letters if it has an inner capital, or if it is an
English word, meaning ASCII letters only, present in the CMU Pronouncing
Dictionary⁴⁴ 4
[https://github.com/cmusphinx/cmudict](https://github.com/cmusphinx/cmudict)
and absent from the word list. The respelling comes from the word’s
pronunciation in that dictionary, and these automatic outputs have not
had a native review.

#### Lexicon.

Automatic respelling is not always better than the written form, so we
checked 100 candidate brand and English terms. We generated each term
both as written and respelled, in one carrier sentence, and transcribed
both with Duyu. A native-speaker author listened to both versions of
every term and decided 94 of them, giving a lexicon of 17 respelled and
77 unchanged terms; the other 6 were left to the automatic rule. Users
can add their own entries. Table 3 gives the scores: for the 17
respelled terms, recognition similarity rises from 0.63 to 0.99. We used
this study to build the lexicon, not to test it. Each form of a term has
one render, and a rule using these same scores proposed 16 of the 17
respellings, so the rise is partly built in.

|                                              |            |           |
| -------------------------------------------- | ---------- | --------- |
|                                              | As written | Respelled |
| Mean similarity, all 100 terms               | 0.911      | 0.961     |
| Transcript equals the term or its respelling | 72         | 85        |
| Mean similarity, the 17 respelled terms      | 0.627      | 0.989     |

Table 3: Recognition of 100 brand and English terms by Duyu, in one
carrier sentence with one voice and seed. Similarity is between the part
of the transcript that fills the term’s slot and either the term or its
respelling, whichever is higher (0–1) $`\uparrow`$.

#### Effect of normalizing at all.

Normalization is not optional for Alania-2. The companion paper tests
this on 150 constructed sentences in the style of clinic and
customer-service messages, covering numbers, dates, units, phone
numbers, initialisms, brands and addresses. Most of their expected
spoken forms have not had a native review. We ran the model directly,
with one voice and one render per sentence, and transcribed the audio
with Duyu. Given raw written text, the model had a word error rate of
66.7%, against 6.4% with normalized text. A critical span is a part of a
sentence that must survive, such as an amount or a name. Every critical
span was recovered from the transcript in 7% of sentences with raw text
and in 81% with normalized text. Among the 96 sentences containing
digits, this held for none with raw text and for 86 with normalized
text. In the transcripts, numbers come out as other words. Nobody
listened to these renders, so this description rests on transcripts and
audio length.

This describes Alania-2, which was trained on normalized text, not
neural TTS in general. Zero-shot VoxCPM2, which generated most of
Alania-2’s training audio, did not break down on raw text in a separate
evaluation. Over 24 prompts and two seeds, its character error rate
(CER) was 10.9% on raw text against 5.6% on normalized text on prompts
built to stress normalization, and 7.7% against 5.5% on numeric prompts.
That evaluation used other prompts, another recognizer and another
scorer, so these figures cannot be set against the ones above number by
number, but they suggest that the failure on digits is not inherent to
the architecture.

#### Effect of respelling.

Respelling adds a smaller gain whose size is uncertain, on terms already
used to build the lexicon. Among the sentences whose text it changes,
every critical span was recovered in 12 of 15 with respelling and 3 of
15 without it through our speech API, and in 11 and 6 of 16 when we ran
the model directly.

## 5  Model

### 5.1  Choosing an architecture

We compared candidate architectures on 120 Turkish prompts with two
seeds each (Figure 3). Zero-shot VoxCPM2 was the most intelligible (CER
0.016), the most natural by UTMOS (3.95) and the most consistent between
renders of the same voice (speaker similarity 0.83). Unlike Alania-1,
which ran on past the end of the text in 7.5% of renders, it never did.
Its VAE also preserved speaker identity through encoding and decoding
far better than the SNAC codec (similarity 0.93 against 0.71). We
therefore adopted the VoxCPM architecture.

Figure 3: Choosing an architecture: intelligibility against naturalness
on 120 Turkish prompts, 240 renders per system. The best systems sit at

### 5.2  Architecture and training

Alania-2 keeps VoxCPM2’s text tokenizer and its frozen audio VAE (Zhou
et al., 2026), which encodes 16 kHz audio into 64-dimensional latents at
25 frames per second and decodes them to 48 kHz. A language-model
backbone reads the text and the latents generated so far. A diffusion
transformer then predicts the next patch of four latent frames (0.16 s
of audio) in 10 flow-matching steps with classifier-free guidance. Our
model is smaller than VoxCPM2 (Table 4), and we train all its other
weights from random initialization with the VoxCPM training code.

| Component         | Layers $`\times`$ width | Heads    | Params |
| ----------------- | ----------------------- | -------- | ------ |
| Backbone LM       | 16 $`\times`$ 1024      | 8 (2 KV) | 268M   |
| Residual LM       | 6 $`\times`$ 1024       | 8 (2 KV) | 72M    |
| Feature encoder   | 8 $`\times`$ 768        | 6        | 69M    |
| Diffusion decoder | 8 $`\times`$ 768        | 6        | 72M    |
| Total             |                         |          | 488M   |

Table 4: Alania-2 architecture, excluding the frozen audio VAE.
Attention heads stay 128-dimensional to match VoxCPM’s position
encoding.

We pursued two tracks in parallel. Track 1 continued training the full
VoxCPM2 on our data (2.3B parameters in the released checkpoint,
excluding the VAE). Track 2 trained our own smaller model from scratch.
A model trained from scratch needs many steps before it learns to align
text with audio: a 1.24B-parameter variant never did within 54k steps
(CER 0.84–0.88), while the 488M model did. The released model was
trained in three stages:

1.  1
    about 64k steps on an earlier 8,904-hour mix;
2.  2
    70k steps on the 18,195-hour base mix, with a batch of 32 per GPU,
    two gradient-accumulation steps, a learning rate of $`10^{-4}`$ and
    weight decay of 0.05;
3.  3
    the second stage on design and style data at a learning rate of
    $`5\times 10^{-5}`$, stopped at step 4,000.

Training took on the order of 210 H200 GPU-hours, not counting data
generation.

![](arxiv-2611-13845v1--0499c66acb76.figures/inline-4.svg)

Figure 4: CER of the candidate models on our 292-clip suite (one seed;
Whisper large-v3), lower is better. On its built-in voices, Alania-2 is
close to the 2.3B models at under a quarter of their size, and the
second training stage fixed most of the base model’s errors with style

### 5.3  Comparison and choice

Figure 4 compares the candidates. On the two built-in voices, Alania-2’s
CER is within half a point of VoxCPM2 and of both Track 1 models. With
style instructions its CER is higher (3.4% against 2.0–2.2% for Track
1). It produced the requested gender for 30 of 32 voice designs, as
often as any candidate. Its UTMOS is 0.2–0.3 lower than VoxCPM2’s, and
it is weaker at cloning a voice from an English recording (Section 8).
We chose Alania-2 because it is far cheaper to serve at nearly the same
intelligibility: on one H200, it keeps up with real time at 12 requests
per second, against about 8 for Track 1.

### 5.4  Voices, styles and design

The two built-in voices are designed voices, not recordings of real
people. We chose them in a blind comparison of 12 candidates. For each
voice we store a reference clip, an average speaker embedding and a
pitch range (199.6–238.6 Hz for the female voice, 109.1–131.6 Hz for the
male voice). At serving time, we use the pitch range to reject renders
that drift away from the voice.

Style control comes from the captioned training rows. At first, the
model sometimes read the instruction aloud: Turkish instructions on
every take, and long English ones on 4 of 6. We now rewrite each
instruction into a short English tag with a small LLM, after which all
54 test takes were clean.

Ready-made styles (warm, calm, energetic, smiling) keep CER between
0.33% and 1.82% and widen the pitch range by 0.5–1.4 semitones.
Whispering and slow speech are beyond the model. For voice design we use
a guidance scale of 3.0, chosen from a sweep between 1.5 and 4.0 in
which higher guidance raised UTMOS (from 1.79 to 2.93) but lowered
gender accuracy (from 30 to 27 of 32).

## 6  Evaluation

### 6.1  Freya-TR-Eval

Freya-TR-Eval (Pamuk et al., 2026) is a public Turkish benchmark of 495
sentences of 3–13 words, given as raw written text. It measures
intelligibility: each system reads every sentence, a speech recognizer
transcribes the audio, and the benchmark reports the word error rate
(WER) against the original text. Its released file contains no digits,
symbols, initialisms or mixed-case brand names, so it does not exercise
text normalization. We re-ran it for 15 systems under one protocol, plus
the bare Alania-2 model without our serving checks, and released the
harness and results. Appendix B lists the exact checkpoint of every
system.⁵⁵ 5
[https://huggingface.co/datasets/PatientdeskAI/freya-tr-eval-rerun](https://huggingface.co/datasets/PatientdeskAI/freya-tr-eval-rerun)
Each system reads every sentence three times. We band-limit the audio to
8 kHz, resample it to 16 kHz, transcribe it with Whisper large-v3
(Radford et al., 2023) through faster-whisper⁶⁶ 6
[https://github.com/SYSTRAN/faster-whisper](https://github.com/SYSTRAN/faster-whisper)
(beam size 5) and score it with the benchmark’s own text normalization.
We also report UTMOS22 on the full-band audio and the real-time factor
on one L4 GPU.

Figure 5 shows the results. Through our API, Alania-2 reaches 1.44% WER,
seventh of the 15 systems. The two best are compact models of under 9M
parameters: EMA Lightning at 1.04% and our Antalia-2 Mini at 1.05%. The
benchmark authors report 1.43% for ElevenLabs v4, which we did not run.
Differences of a tenth of a point are within the spread between the
three readings. Our serving checks and retries (Section 9) lower the
bare model’s 1.51% to 1.44%. Compared with Alania-1, Alania-2 cuts WER
four-fold and character error rate (CER) six-fold (Table 5).

#### System version.

This report describes release alania-v2 of October 2026, with two
built-in voices and 10 diffusion steps. The service has changed since
then. Sampling moved to 6 diffusion steps, and on 11 October release
alania-v2.1, a fine-tune of alania-v2 for control of speaking style with
the same architecture, replaced the model. On 12 October nine voices
were added. The Freya-TR-Eval results here use the female voice
(alania2-f1) for Alania-2 and version 1.0.0 of Antalia-2 Mini; our
released results also hold later runs of both.

### 6.2  What we did not measure

This report does not yet include a listening test with many listeners.
Decisions that needed listening, such as choosing the architecture and
the built-in voices, were made in blind comparisons by one native
speaker, and our only naturalness measure is UTMOS22, which was trained
on English. Intelligibility results depend on the recognizer and on how
text is normalized before scoring, so we name the recognizer with every
result.

## 7  How Large Does the Model Need to Be?

Alania-2 has 488M parameters, while open speech models of several
billion parameters are available, so it is fair to ask why we did not
build on a larger one. We did try: in our experiments, the larger models
were barely more intelligible, and a far smaller model trained with the
same recipe matched the best systems we measured.

### 7.1  Larger models trained on our data

Table 6 compares Alania-2 with the larger models from Section 5.
Continuing to train the full VoxCPM2, at 2.3B parameters, improved CER
on the built-in voices by only 0.3–0.5 points over Alania-2. The larger
model did keep clear advantages elsewhere. It followed style
instructions more accurately (CER 2.0% against 3.4%), it sounded
somewhat more natural (UTMOS 0.16 higher on the female voice), and it
cloned voices from English recordings far better (similarity 0.64
against 0.41, measured before Alania-2’s second training stage).
Training a larger model from scratch did not help either: our
1.24B-parameter variant had still not learned to align text with audio
after 54k steps, when the 488M model already had. At serving time, the
smaller model handles 1.5 times as many requests per GPU.

### 7.2  Size and intelligibility across systems

The same holds across the systems in our Freya-TR-Eval re-run
(Figure 6). Model size does not predict word error rate: the three most
intelligible systems with published sizes span 7.6M to 2.4B parameters,
and our 3.3B-parameter Alania-1 misreads more than four times as many
words as the 488M Alania-2. Training data and other design choices
separate these systems far more than size does. This benchmark cannot
show the effect of text handling, because its sentences contain no
digits, initialisms or mixed-case brand names.

### 7.3  Going much smaller

To test how far the recipe scales down, we trained Antalia-2 Mini, an
openly released model with a single voice. It has 7.62M parameters: a
3.69M-parameter acoustic model that generates mel spectrograms with
few-step flow matching, and a 3.93M-parameter vocoder that produces
48 kHz audio. Like Alania-2, it learns from synthetic speech, here about
1,790 hours of one voice generated by VoxCPM2, and it reads text through
the same normalizer (Section 4). On Freya-TR-Eval it reaches 1.05% WER
and 0.20% CER, the lowest CER of the 15 systems we ran. Its teacher,
VoxCPM2 speaking the same voice, scored 0.92% WER and 0.20% CER in a
single run on our development harness. A model about 300 times smaller
thus keeps nearly all of its teacher’s intelligibility.

Antalia-2 Mini also shows what the extra parameters pay for. It has one
voice and no cloning, voice design or style control, and it sounds less
natural than Alania-2 (UTMOS 3.25 against 3.67). For reading plain
Turkish sentences in a fixed voice, a few million parameters suffice
when the data are right. The larger capacity of Alania-2 goes into
flexibility: many voices, cloning from a short recording, voice design
and style instructions. We chose 488M as the smallest size we tried that
kept these abilities while serving the most requests per GPU.

## 8  Voice Cloning

Alania-2 offers two kinds of cloned voice. An instant clone is
conditioned on a short reference recording at request time. A
professional clone is a LoRA adapter trained for one voice on one to
five minutes of speech. The reference recordings in this section come
from consenting contributors to a Turkish speech corpus and from FLEURS
(Conneau et al., 2023). We measure CER and skipped words with Whisper
large-v3 and word alignment, and speaker similarity as the cosine
similarity of ECAPA embeddings between the generated audio and a
held-out recording of the same speaker.

### 8.1  Continuing a reference skips words

VoxCPM can use a reference recording in two ways. In continuation mode,
the reference audio and its transcript come before the new text, and the
model continues the recording. In reference-only mode, the reference
audio sets the voice and no transcript is given.

We first served clones in continuation mode, and users reported skipped
words, silent gaps and loops. With references of 20 s or more,
continuation skipped about half of every text, often whole chunks, and a
wrong reference transcript had the same effect at any length. Switching
to reference-only mode, together with per-chunk checks (Section 9),
fixed this without losing speaker identity (Figure 7). Over 200 streams
with the same seeds and texts, skipped words fell from 21.9% to 1.7%,
and to 0.18% on clean references. Speaker similarity on clean references
was unchanged (0.569 against 0.568). The near-silent chunks that users
heard as gaps disappeared (from 45 to 0), and the longest pause where
two chunks join mid-sentence fell from 1.04 s to 0.37 s.

![](arxiv-2611-13845v1--0499c66acb76.figures/inline-7.svg)

Figure 7: Streamed instant clones before and after the fix; lower is
better. 200 streams each, with the same seeds and texts of 121–249
characters. “Hard” references contain reverberation, music, a laptop
microphone, noise or MP3 compression.

### 8.2  How much reference audio helps

Figure 8 compares the two modes as the reference grows from 5 to 120 s
(four speakers, three texts, three seeds). In reference-only mode,
similarity rises from 0.56 at 5 s to 0.64–0.67 at 20–30 s. Beyond that,
the model breaks down, with a CER of 0.47 at 60 s and 0.90 at 120 s.
Continuation mode is already unusable at 20 s. Choosing the cleanest
20-second window of a recording is better than taking its first 20
seconds (similarity 0.672 against 0.637, CER 0.024 against 0.032). We
therefore accept references of 3–30 s, recommend 20–30 s, and
automatically pick the cleanest 20-second window from longer uploads.
Encoding the reference takes 31 ms at 5 s and 404 ms at 120 s, while
first audio stays between 83 and 114 ms.

8![](arxiv-2611-13845v1--0499c66acb76.figures/inline-8.svg)
Figure 8: Instant clones as the reference grows, using the first $`N`$
seconds of each recording. Reference-only mode is stable up to 30 s.

#### Chaining chunks does not help.

We also tried conditioning each chunk of a long text on the end of the
previous chunk. Over 80 paired renders, this lowered CER by 0.035–0.069
but also lowered similarity by 0.058–0.074, and the voice drifted from
chunk to chunk. Every chunk therefore stays conditioned on the original
reference.

#### Reference quality.

We convert saved references to mono 48 kHz at $`-20`$ dBFS and check
them for background noise, echo, clipping and implausible transcripts.
Background noise only 12 dB below the speech made the model skip 29–49%
of words.

### 8.3  Professional clones

A professional clone is a rank-32 LoRA adapter (Hu et al., 2022) on the
backbone and the diffusion decoder. The training pipeline splits the
upload into 3–15 s utterances with a signal-to-noise ratio above 15 dB,
transcribes them, holds out 10% for evaluation, and trains for 200 steps
on 80 s of speech, rising linearly to 400 steps for five minutes. The
adapter is used only if it beats the instant clone made from the best
20-second reference on the held-out speech: similarity must rise by at
least 0.02, and CER must stay below $`\max(0.05,1.5c)`$, where $`c`$ is
the instant clone’s CER.

Training takes 84–126 s on one A100, and a whole job finishes in 3–4.5
minutes. Figure 9 shows large similarity gains in every run. One
speaker’s adapter trained on 580 s of speech (similarity 0.68) scored
below the same speaker’s 78-second adapter (0.72), which suggests that
matching the recording session matters more than the amount of audio at
this scale. Cloning a voice from an English recording into Turkish
speech remains weak: similarity 0.41, against 0.64 for Track 1, measured
before the second training stage.

![](arxiv-2611-13845v1--0499c66acb76.figures/inline-9.svg)

### 8.4  Verification

Because a professional clone reproduces a voice closely, we require the
voice’s owner to be present. The speaker reads a random Turkish sentence
aloud in the browser. The sentence is drawn from about three million
combinations and expires after 15 minutes. Duyu must recognize the
sentence, and the speaker embedding of the reading must match the
uploaded recordings. On 13 consenting speakers (84 same-speaker pairs
across recordings and 2,460 different-speaker pairs), a threshold of
0.45 rejects 0.5% of genuine readings and accepts 0.45% of impostor
pairs. In a live test, a different speaker’s reading scored 0.20 and was
rejected within 3 s. Our API also refuses to synthesize any pending
challenge sentence, so the check cannot be passed by generating the
reading with an instant clone through our own service.

## 9  Serving

![](arxiv-2611-13845v1--0499c66acb76.figures/inline-10.svg)

Figure 10: Serving layout on each GPU machine.

Each GPU machine runs the Alania-2 worker and an API front that also
serves Alania-1 (Figure 10). CUDA’s Multi-Process Service (MPS) lets the
two models share the GPU fairly: without it, four Alania-1 streams next
to an Alania-2 load fell from 100% to 3% keeping up with real time; with
it, they held 100% next to 24 Alania-2 streams.

#### From text to audio.

The front normalizes the text (Section 4) and splits it into chunks of
at most 140 characters, preferring sentence and then clause boundaries.
It then resolves the voice (built-in, designed, instant clone or
professional clone) and streams the first chunk as it is generated, in
steps of 0.16 s.

#### Checks and retries.

Later chunks are generated up to two chunks ahead of playback and
checked before they are sent. A chunk is rejected if it is empty; if it
runs on too long (more than 2.0 s plus 0.11 s per character); if its
length does not fit its text; if it contains a pause of more than 1 s;
if it has no voiced speech; or, for the built-in voices, if its median
pitch is outside the voice’s range. A rejected chunk is generated again
with a new seed, up to twice, and the best attempt is kept. If waiting
would make playback stall, the chunk is sent unchecked. When joining
chunks, we trim their edge silence and insert 350 ms of silence after a
sentence and 120 ms after a clause. Every response is labelled as
AI-generated in its headers.

#### Deployment.

We run on spot RTX PRO 6000 machines in one EU region, with on-demand
A100 machines as a floor that is never preempted. A new machine serves
Alania-2 81 s after boot. Professional clones are trained on a separate,
always-on GPU machine in the EU.

## 10  Inference Engine

The worker runs Alania-2 on Nano-vLLM-VoxCPM (a710128, 2025), an
inference engine for VoxCPM based on Nano-vLLM (GeeeekExplorer, 2025).
It batches concurrent streams and keeps the backbone’s key/value cache
in fixed-size blocks (Kwon et al., 2023). We changed the engine in three
places to cut first-audio latency and raise throughput, and in several
more to serve many fine-tuned voices from one model. We checked each
change against the unmodified engine on the same seeds and texts.

### 10.1  Latency and throughput

Table 7 lists the three changes:

- •
  Batched VAE decoding. The audio decoder ran once per stream. Running
  it once per batch cut decoding at 96 streams from 110 to 27 ms per
  step. At 48 streams, throughput rose from 41 to 74 seconds of audio
  per second, and the share of streams that kept ahead of real time rose
  from none to all.
- •
  CUDA-graph prefill. We capture the prompt step as CUDA graphs, as the
  engine already did for later steps. Single-stream first audio fell
  from 74 to 32 ms, and the output stayed bit-identical (23 of 23
  renders).
- •
  Kernel warm-up. A batch size the engine had not seen before stalled
  every stream for about 1.4 s. We now run the common batch sizes once
  at start-up.

### 10.2  Serving many fine-tuned voices

Professional clones (Section 8) are LoRA adapters on one shared base
model, as in S-LoRA (Sheng et al., 2024) and Punica (Chen et al., 2024).
Each request names its adapter. Up to eight adapters stay on the GPU,
and requests with different adapters, or none, share a batch. Adapters
load from storage on first use and are cached in host memory and on
disk. A broken adapter falls back to the instant clone of the same
voice.

#### Prefix-cache correctness.

The engine reuses cached key/value blocks when a new request starts with
the same tokens as an earlier one. Its cache key covered only the
tokens, so a request for a fine-tuned voice could reuse blocks that a
plain request had computed from the same reference and text. We saw 8 of
9 prompt blocks reused this way, and 3 of 3 fine-tuned renders differed
from renders with an empty cache. We now seed the cache key with the
adapter’s name and a digest of its weights. Fine-tuned renders then
match empty-cache renders bit for bit, and plain requests keep their
existing keys.

#### Two smaller fixes.

We replaced a LoRA kernel that would not compile on our Triton version;
outputs agree to within $`2\times 10^{-7}`$. Batches with a single
adapter now run as plain matrix products, which cut the adapter’s
first-audio overhead from 33 to 13 ms.

With these fixes, quality through the engine matches the reference
implementation (Table 8). In mixed batches, every render was closest to
its own speaker (60 of 60), and throughput with every stream on an
adapter is 4–15% below plain clones.

## 11  Limitations

Our evaluation has several gaps. First, naturalness rests on UTMOS22, a
predictor trained on English speech, and on blind comparisons by one
native listener. Second, intelligibility depends on the speech
recognizer and on the text normalization used for scoring, and the same
recognizers also helped build our training data. Third, ECAPA speaker
similarity separates speakers on our data, but differences below about
0.05 should not be over-read: a style instruction alone moves similarity
to the plain voice from about 0.91 to between 0.64 and 0.80. Fourth,
most results come from a single seed or a single run.

The model itself has limits. Ninety percent of its training audio comes
from VoxCPM2, so Alania-2 inherits its teacher’s prosody and artefacts,
including narrow high-frequency tones in some voices that we filter out
of the synthetic data but do not yet suppress at serving time. The base
training text was also normalized before the native-speaker review of
Section 4, so the model now receives some readings, including all brand
respellings, that it never saw in training. Finally, exact GPU-hours and
some early training configurations were not recorded.

## 12  Ethics and Safety

#### Training data.

The real speech comes from public YouTube videos distributed in YODAS
under Creative Commons terms. Our synthetic text includes Wikipedia (CC
BY-SA) and FineWeb2 (ODC-By). The built-in voices are designed voices
that do not belong to any real person, and our released synthetic speech
contains designed voices only. We do not release the model weights.

#### Cloning.

Cloning someone’s voice without their agreement can enable fraud,
harassment and disinformation. Every clone requires the user to state
that they have the speaker’s consent, and we record this statement with
the voice. Professional clones also require the live reading and speaker
match of Section 8, with separate consent to the biometric comparison.
Speaker embeddings are computed in memory and never stored, uploaded
recordings are deleted seven days after training, customer voice data
stays in the EU, and every response is labelled as AI-generated.

These controls have gaps. Instant clones have no live verification, so
only the user’s statement of consent stands in the way of misuse. We do
not yet watermark generated audio (San Roman et al., 2024), and we have
no specific policy on the voices of public figures beyond requiring
consent. Every voice in this report’s cloning experiments belongs to a
consenting contributor or comes from FLEURS.

## 13  Conclusion

We presented Alania-2, a 488M-parameter Turkish TTS model trained mostly
on synthetic speech. On its built-in voices, it comes within half a
point of CER of a fine-tuned model more than four times its size,
generates 48 kHz audio, and starts streaming in tens of milliseconds.
Larger models bought little intelligibility, and a 7.6M-parameter model
trained with the same recipe matched the best systems we measured; extra
parameters pay for flexibility rather than accuracy. Several of the most
useful lessons concerned the system around the model. Alania-2, trained
on normalized text, needs a text normalizer to read digits and
abbreviations. Cloning by continuing a reference fails with long
references, while conditioning on the reference audio alone does not,
and 20–30 seconds of reference audio works best. A low-rank adapter
trained in about two minutes gives a close voice match, and serving many
such adapters needs care with caching. Next, we plan a listening test
with many native listeners, audio watermarking, live verification for
instant clones, and retraining on text from the current normalizer.
